# Evidence: the benchmark matrix (evaluation tests 7 and 8, 2026-10-24)

Throughput and latency of every engine on 1, 2 and 4 instances, over three key distributions.
Script: `bench/run_matrix.sh` (`RUNS=5`, 240 runs, 0 failed calls), tables by
`tools/matrix_tables.py results/2026-10-24_matrix/summary.txt`. Binary: commit c606a5d plus the Zipf
load generator (committed as 0e1f421).

## Setup

- **Engines:** `allow_all` (gRPC with no limiter); `strict` and `leased` on `api_default` (a token
  bucket of 1,000 refilled at 500 a second), leased with chunk caps 1%, 5% and 10%; the `customer`
  check (burst, then daily quota) for Pay customers `cust:pay:1..1000000`.
- **Keys:** uniform over 1,000,000; Zipf with exponent 0.99 over 1,000,000; one hot key.
- **Load:** closed loop, 192 calls in flight in all (12 connections of 16, split over the instances),
  3 s of warm-up, 12 s measured. The instances share the 192, so the matrix measures what N instances
  do with the same machine, not added capacity.
- **Cores:** servers pinned to logical CPUs 0-5, load generators to 6-11. WSL2 shows 6 cores of 2
  threads, so it does not say which of these are the laptop's performance or efficiency cores. Redis
  (3 masters in Docker, no persistence) is not pinned.
- **State:** FLUSHALL before every run of the first three engines. The customer cells ran last, on
  1,000,000 Pay plans seeded once and never flushed between runs, so a customer's quota carries over
  from one run to the next.

## Reading

- **Leases pay where keys repeat.** The gain over strict depends on the key distribution:
  - *Uniform over 1,000,000 keys: none.* 42,073 against 42,065. A run makes about 600,000 checks
    over 1,000,000 keys, so a key is seen less than once and nearly every check is a lease miss that
    goes to Redis like a strict check.
  - *Zipf: +55% to +66%* (62,679 to 67,459 against 40,571), with p99 halved (6.9 against 12.6 ms).
  - *One hot key: 4.5 to 5.2 times strict* (82,923 to 95,913 against 18,386), and as fast as gRPC
    with no limiter, within noise. That comparison is not like for like, because `allow_all` ran on
    uniform keys.
- **A larger chunk cap is a little faster when keys repeat** (Zipf: 62,679 at 1%, 67,459 at 10%),
  because it needs fewer refills. That is the price of the tighter accuracy bound of
  `burst_bound_2026-10-22.md`. On uniform keys the caps differ within run-to-run noise: the medians
  are 46,021, 45,128 and 44,904 on 2 instances, and the 10% cell on 1 instance spans 21,294 to 38,307.
- **One hot key on the strict path is bound by one connection, not by Redis.** One instance sends a
  key's calls through one of its four Redis clients, which has one connection and one I/O thread:
  18,386 checks a second. Two instances use two connections to the same master and reach 46,283.
- **More instances on one laptop add no capacity, and none is expected:**
  - *Why:* the instances share the 192 calls in flight and the 6 server CPUs.
  - *2 against 1:* two instances beat one on most cells (`allow_all`: 82,704 to 108,946), because one
    process does not use its 6 CPUs fully (348% against 397%).
  - *4 against 2:* four lose again (98,419), competing for the same CPUs.
  - *What it shows:* no engine falls apart with more instances; scaling itself needs more machines
    (section 3.8 and Chapter 5's extrapolation).
  - *The customer check loses most, 1 to 4 instances: −28%* (25,867 to 18,703, against −12% for
    strict). A likely cause, not measured here: each instance has its own plan cache, so with N
    instances each sees a customer 1/N as often and reads its plan more often.

## The customer check on one hot key

The five runs measured three different states of one customer, `cust:pay:1`. Its quota carried over
from the Zipf cells, where it was the most frequent key.

| Run | Checks/s | Allowed/s | p50 | State |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 14,365 | 20 | 13.8 ms | over its burst (20 a second), within quota: every call runs the burst script on one master |
| 2 | 36,127 | 2,506 | 2.9 ms | crossed its daily quota during the run |
| 3 to 5 | 84,106 to 90,754 | 0 | 2.0 to 2.1 ms | out of quota: the deny cache answers locally |

The median (84,106) is therefore the deny cache's speed, not a typical hot customer's.

**Run 2 allowed more than the quota.** It allowed about 30,000 calls in its 12 s window, yet Redis
counted 2,200 for the day (`used 2200`, exactly the plan's 2,000 plus 200 grace). In the code, the
only allowed answer that Redis does not count is the fail-open one. A paid plan's call gets it, as
`UNVERIFIED`, when Redis times out or its breaker is open. So these were almost certainly fail-open
answers during a Redis stall, but the run had metrics off and its raw output was deleted, so neither
the stall nor its cause was observed.

It did not happen again in 11 attempts on fresh Pay customers, each crossing the daily quota:
- *8 runs with metrics on:* every allowed answer was counted in Redis (`ok` equal to the change in
  `used`), with no deadline misses and no breaker trip.
- *3 runs with metrics off:* allowed in the window was at most the change in `used`.

**Since then** the load generator counts `UNVERIFIED` answers in the closed loop, and the matrix
records them, so a run like this one is visible.

**This is a risk the design accepts** (3.1.1): while its master stalls, a paid customer is allowed
without being counted. Abuse would need the customer to stall the master itself. One customer's
over-burst flood did not do so here (run 1, and `noisy_neighbour_2026-10-23.md`).

## CheckBatch on one instance (2026-10-24)

The leased rule over Zipf 1M keys, as in the matrix, but 10 checks per call (`CheckBatch`), 3 runs:
338,385, 407,896 and 400,187 checks per second (33,839 to 40,790 calls a second), p99 12.1 to 19.5 ms,
no failed call. The median, 400,187, is 5.5 times single calls on the same workload, which supports
the profile's reading (`profile_2026-10-24.md`): the cost per check is mostly gRPC's cost per call.
Since each call carries 10 checks, the latency is per call of 10.

## Redis Functions against EVALSHA (2026-10-24)

`bench/redis_functions.sh` (`RUNS=5`, the throwaway test Redis 7.4.11): `lua/token_bucket.lua` loaded
once as a script (`EVALSHA`) and once, unchanged, as the body of a function (`FCALL`), driven by
redis-benchmark over 100,000 random keys, 50 clients.

| Pipeline | EVALSHA calls/s (range) | FCALL calls/s (range) | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 62,469 (54,549 to 66,631) | 62,461 (47,842 to 66,622) | 0.0% |
| 16 | 142,572 (96,956 to 142,613) | 142,613 (124,719 to 142,613) | 0.0% |

The same Lua runs either way; Functions change how scripts are loaded and named, not their cost per
call. With pipelining, both stop at the same ceiling, set by the benchmark or Docker's networking rather
than by either command. Not adopted: `EVALSHA` with a fallback to `EVAL` on `NOSCRIPT` already copes with a
restarted node.
